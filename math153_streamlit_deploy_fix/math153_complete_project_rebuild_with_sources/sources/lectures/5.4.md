# 5.4 --- Logarithmic Functions

## Logarithms as Inverse Functions

The notes recall that every one-to-one function has an inverse.

For the exponential function

\[ f(x)=a\^x, \]

the inverse is

\[ `\boxed{f^{-1}(x)=\log_a(x)}`{=tex}. \]

Therefore

\[ (f`\circ `{=tex}f\^{-1})(x)=x \]

gives

\[ `\boxed{a^{\log_a(x)}=x}`{=tex}, \]

and

\[ (f\^{-1}`\circ `{=tex}f)(x)=x \]

gives

\[ `\boxed{\log_a(a^x)=x}`{=tex}. \]

The fundamental equivalence is

\[ `\boxed{\log_a(x)=y\iff a^y=x}`{=tex}. \]

## Converting Between Forms

Example:

\[ 5\^2=u \]

becomes

\[ `\boxed{\log_5(u)=2}`{=tex}. \]

Example:

\[ w=`\log`{=tex}\_4(2t+3) \]

becomes

\[ `\boxed{4^w=2t+3}`{=tex}. \]

## Basic Logarithm Values

\[ `\log`{=tex}\_{10}(100)=2 \]

\[ `\log`{=tex}\_2(2)=1 \]

\[ `\log`{=tex}\_2(4)=2 \]

## One-to-One Property

Logarithmic functions are decreasing when

\[ 0\<a\<1 \]

and increasing when

\[ a\>1. \]

Thus they are one-to-one.

Example:

\[ `\log`{=tex}\_6(4x-5)=`\log`{=tex}\_6(2x+1). \]

Since the logarithm is one-to-one,

\[ 4x-5=2x+1. \]

Thus

\[ 2x=6 \]

and

\[ `\boxed{x=3}`{=tex}. \]

Check:

\[ `\log`{=tex}\_6(7)=`\log`{=tex}\_6(7). \]

### Extraneous-Solution Example

\[ `\log`{=tex}\_4(x+1)=`\log`{=tex}\_4(2x+3). \]

Equating the arguments gives

\[ x+1=2x+3 \]

and hence

\[ x=-2. \]

But substitution produces logarithms of negative numbers, so (x=-2) is
extraneous.

## Common Log and Natural Log

Common logarithm:

\[ `\boxed{\log(x)=\log_{10}(x)}`{=tex}. \]

Natural logarithm:

\[ `\boxed{\ln(x)=\log_e(x)}`{=tex}. \]

## More Conversions

Convert

\[ 10\^{y+3}=z \]

to logarithmic form:

\[ `\boxed{y+3=\log(z)}`{=tex}. \]

Convert

\[ `\ln`{=tex}(x)=2 \]

to exponential form:

\[ `\boxed{x=e^2}`{=tex}. \]

# Applications

## Richter Scale

The notes use

\[ `\boxed{R=\log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)}`{=tex}, \]

where (I_0) is a certain minimum intensity.

If

\[ I=1000I_0, \]

then

\[ R=`\log`{=tex}(1000)=3. \]

Thus

\[ `\boxed{R=3}`{=tex}. \]

To express (I) in terms of (R) and (I_0):

\[
R=`\log`{=tex}\_{10}`\left`{=tex}(`\frac{I}{I_0}`{=tex}`\right`{=tex})
\]

implies

\[ 10\^R=`\frac{I}{I_0}`{=tex}. \]

Therefore

\[ `\boxed{I=I_0\,10^R}`{=tex}. \]

## Continuous Growth and Doubling Time

Assume a population grows continuously at (4%) per year:

\[ P(t)=P_0e\^{rt}, `\qquad `{=tex}r=0.04. \]

At the doubling time (t_2),

\[ P(t_2)=2P(0). \]

This gives

\[ e\^{rt_2}=2. \]

Take natural logarithms:

\[ rt_2=`\ln`{=tex}(2). \]

Therefore the general doubling-time formula is

\[ `\boxed{t_2=\frac{\ln(2)}{r}}`{=tex}. \]

For (r=0.04),

\[
t_2=`\frac{\ln(2)}{0.04}`{=tex}`\approx`{=tex}`\boxed{17.33\text{ years}}`{=tex}.
\]

## Radioactive Decay and Half-Life

A physicist records (2000) counts per minute initially and (1500) counts
per minute after (10) days.

The model is

\[ N(t)=ke\^{ct}. \]

At (t=0),

\[ N(0)=k, \]

so

\[ `\boxed{k=2000}`{=tex}. \]

Thus

\[ N(t)=2000e\^{ct}. \]

Using (N(10)=1500):

\[ 1500=2000e\^{10c} \]

\[ `\frac34`{=tex}=e\^{10c}. \]

Take natural logarithms:

\[ `\ln`{=tex}`\left`{=tex}(`\frac34`{=tex}`\right`{=tex})=10c. \]

Therefore

\[ `\boxed{c=\frac{\ln(3/4)}{10}\approx-0.029}`{=tex}. \]

The notes describe this as a decay rate of approximately (2.9%).

For half-life (t\_{1/2}),

\[ N(t\_{1/2})=`\frac`{=tex}12N(0). \]

Thus

\[ e\^{ct\_{1/2}}=`\frac12`{=tex} \]

and

\[ ct\_{1/2}=`\ln`{=tex}`\left`{=tex}(`\frac12`{=tex}`\right`{=tex}). \]

The general half-life formula is

\[ `\boxed{
t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln(1/2)}{c}
}`{=tex}. \]

With (c`\approx-0.029`{=tex}),

\[ `\boxed{t_{1/2}\approx23.9\text{ years}}`{=tex} \]

as written in the notes.
